What this subject actually asks of you

Business Management looks like the readable Group 3 option. It is readable. That is not the same as being easy to score in. This guide is about the gap between those two things, and it was written by a DP2 student who takes the subject at Higher Level alongside Economics HL.

What this guide covers, and what it does not

The unit pages cover Unit 1 (Introduction to Business Management), Unit 3 (Finance and Accounts) and Unit 4 (Marketing). That is three of the five units, chosen because they carry most of the content DP1 students meet first and struggle with most. Unit 2 (Human Resource Management) and Unit 5 (Operations Management) appear on the course map so you can see where they fit, but they are not taught here.

One thing to check

Schools order the units differently, and syllabus details get revised from time to time. Treat this guide as a way of thinking about the subject. Your teacher and the current IB subject guide are the authority on what is examinable.

A DP2 student's view

Business Management vs Economics

Both sit in Group 3. Both use the words demand, market and cost. Almost every student who picks between them picks on the wrong basis, because the two subjects differ far less in content than in what they ask you to do with it.

The view this section argues

Economics behaves like a mathematics subject. There is a fixed logic that always runs the same way, so the variance is low, and a high mark comes from learning that logic and explaining concepts through it. Business Management hands you an unseen case study and asks you to write whatever that particular case demands. The variance is much higher. What separates a 7 from a 5 is flexible critical thinking, not recall.

Low variance versus high variance

In Economics, once you can build the diagram and follow the chain of reasoning, most questions on that topic resolve the same way. The question changes and the logic does not. That is why Economics rewards drilling. You are building one reliable procedure and applying it over and over.

Business Management removes that safety. A Paper 1 case study is a business you have never seen, in an industry you may know nothing about, with constraints invented for that paper. The concept you need might be sources of finance, but the answer depends on whether the owner cares more about keeping control than growing quickly, and you can only learn that from the stimulus in front of you. No procedure survives the change of case. Only judgement does.

This is why students who bring their Economics habits into Business Management underperform. They write technically correct paragraphs about a concept in the abstract, and lose the application and evaluation marks completely.

The overlap does not transfer

Several concepts genuinely appear in both subjects. Demand, market structure, economies of scale, costs and revenue. It is tempting to assume that studying one gives you a head start in the other. In practice the direction in which each subject applies the shared concept is so different that taking one does not necessarily help you in the other.

The same concept, two different jobs

Economies of scale in Economics. A movement along the long-run average cost curve, explained through the diagram, used to reason about efficiency in a market.

Economies of scale in Business Management. A reason why this particular firm's cost per unit would fall if it grew, which you then weigh against diseconomies, the cost of financing that growth, and whether growth even matches what the owner said they want.

Write the first version in a Business Management answer and you get the knowledge mark and nothing else. There is no diagram to draw, and the marker is looking for the named organisation.

So if you take both, keep them in separate boxes. Shared vocabulary is not shared method.

The differences that show up in an exam room

  • Economics gives you tools you must draw. Business Management gives you tools you must choose between, like SWOT, STEEPLE, Ansoff, position maps and the Boston Consulting Group matrix. Choosing the wrong one for the case wastes half your answer.
  • Economics questions are self-contained. Business Management questions are attached to a stimulus you have to mine, so reading speed and note-taking under time pressure are part of the skill being tested.
  • Business Management asks you to decide. Words like recommend, justify and to what extent require a position. An answer that lays out both sides and then stops is not a complete answer.
A DP2 student's view

Who should take this subject

The honest version, including the students who regret it. If either of those descriptions fits you, that is worth knowing in the first week of DP1 rather than in the second year.

The short answer

Take Business Management if you can build an argument in writing. That is the single ability the subject pays for. Students who choose it expecting to memorise their way to a good grade, and students for whom writing extended English is a burden, are the two groups who most often regret it.

Choose it if you can argue on paper

Almost every mark above the definition level comes from a sentence in which you take a position and support it. Not a sentence that reports what a concept is. A sentence that says this business should do this, for this reason, under this condition. If you already enjoy that kind of writing, or you are willing to be trained into it, the subject suits you and the ceiling is high.

You do not need business experience beforehand. You do not need to have taken IGCSE Business. What you need is the willingness to commit to a judgement and defend it, over and over, under time pressure.

Two ways to regret this choice

You plan to win on memorisation

Why it fails

Definitions are worth real marks, but they are the [2] openers, not the paper. Once the command term moves to analyse, discuss or evaluate, the marker is checking whether you can use the concept on a business you have never seen. Perfect recall with no application produces a stable ceiling around a 4 or 5, and no amount of extra memorising moves it.

The fix, if you stay

Shift your revision from learning the content to writing answers on unseen cases. The content is the smaller half of this subject.

Writing extended English is a burden

Why it fails

Paper 2 at Higher Level is 50 marks in 1 hour 45 minutes, all of it written under time pressure with stimulus material to read first. Writing speed and clarity are not incidental to the subject. They are part of what is being measured. A student who knows the content but writes slowly runs out of paper before running out of ideas.

The fix, if you stay

Train sentence patterns rather than vocabulary. The answer templates page gives fixed stems you can reuse in every answer, which removes most of the composition cost under exam conditions.

Things that are true, but are not reasons to avoid it

  • "There is maths in it." There is, but it is bounded. A formula booklet is provided in the examination, and break-even analysis now sits in Unit 5.5 rather than in Finance. The quantitative demand is far below Economics HL, let alone Mathematics AA. See the formula booklet page.
  • "I have never studied business." The syllabus assumes no prior knowledge. IGCSE Business helps a little with vocabulary and not at all with the skill being tested.
  • "The Internal Assessment sounds hard." It is self-directed. You write 1,800 words built on real supporting documents you find yourself, and it is worth 20% at HL and 30% at SL. That is a reason to start it seriously, not a reason to avoid the subject.

A quick self-check

Answer honestly. These are the questions that actually predict the grade.

  • When you finish reading an article about a company, can you say what you think it should do next, and why?
  • Are you comfortable writing 250 to 300 words of argued English in about twenty minutes?
  • When two sensible options exist, can you commit to one instead of concluding that both have advantages?
  • Are you willing to be told your answer was well written but earned no application marks, and to change how you write because of it?

Three or four yeses is a strong signal. Three or four noes is worth a conversation with your coordinator now, while changing subjects is still cheap.

Paper 1, the one you see in advance

About three months before the examination, the IB releases a statement containing research topics to investigate, plus roughly the first 200 words of the case study. The full case runs to around 800 or 1,000 words and appears only in the exam room.

Section A is structured questions worth 20 marks in total. Section B is one extended response chosen from two options, worth 10 marks. Most questions are qualitative, though a small calculation can turn up.

What this means in DP1

The pre-release rewards breadth. You cannot predict which unit the extended response will draw on, so a student with solid working knowledge across all units has a real advantage over one who revised three units deeply. That is the argument for keeping a definition bank running all year instead of cramming before each test.

Paper 2, quantitative focus

Stimulus material comes in parts, mixing written text with charts and infographics, and you answer as you work through it. Section A is structured questions worth 20 marks. Section B is a structured question chosen from two options, worth 20 marks. At Higher Level there is an additional Section C, an extended response based on a case study, which takes the HL total to 50 marks.

Quantitative focus does not mean the paper is arithmetic. Calculations appear, but the marks sit in what you say about the number you produced. A correct ratio with no interpretation is a fraction of the available marks.

Paper 3, social enterprise HL

A single compulsory question worth 25 marks, based on an unseen social enterprise presented through short introductions, visuals and document excerpts. The question climbs the assessment objectives in stages. First describing human needs, then explaining the challenges a social entrepreneur faces, then recommending a course of action supported by the evidence you were given.

Because it is entirely unseen and entirely about social enterprise, this paper is the clearest test of the flexible thinking the subject is built around. There is no content shortcut for it.

Internal Assessment, the business research project

Around 20 hours of work producing 1,800 words plus three to five supporting documents that you source yourself. The project applies business tools to a real organisation through one of the four course concepts: change, creativity, ethics or sustainability.

The DP1 decision that matters

The most common Internal Assessment failure is choosing an organisation you cannot get documents about. Start collecting candidate organisations in DP1, and before you commit, check that you can actually obtain three to five usable supporting documents. Everything else about the project is fixable. That is not.

Where did break-even go?

If you are using an older textbook or an older set of notes, break-even analysis sits under Finance. Not any more. It is now 5.5, in Operations Management. Reading the HL badge

Higher Level is not Standard Level plus extra units. It is the same five units with additional topics inside them, plus Paper 3 and a longer Paper 2. That is why an HL student cannot simply revise the HL topics separately. The extensions are woven into units you already study.

How to study for a 7

Command terms decide everything

The topic tells you what to write about. The command term tells you what the answer has to be. Students who lose marks in this subject almost always know the content. They wrote the wrong shape of answer.

The rule to internalise first

Read the command term and the mark allocation before you read the rest of the question. Together they fix the length, the structure, and whether you need a judgement, all before you have thought about the topic at all.

The mark ladder

Mark allocations are set per paper and do vary, but the relationship between marks and expected shape is stable enough to plan around.

MarksTypical command termsWhat the answer must contain
[2]define state identify outline calculateOne accurate sentence, or a number with its working shown. No development, no examples needed.
[4]explain distinguish suggest applyTwo developed points, or one point developed with application to the organisation. Reasoning, not a list.
[6]analyse examine commentChains of reasoning tied to the stimulus. Both sides start to appear. Still no formal judgement required.
[10]evaluate discuss to what extent recommend justifyBalanced argument, application throughout, and a supported judgement that answers the question asked.

A [10] answer written in the shape of a [4] answer is the most expensive single mistake in this subject. It is well written, it is correct, and it is capped at roughly half marks.

One topic, four questions

Here is the same content, sources of finance for a small manufacturer, asked four ways. Notice how little the knowledge changes and how completely the answer does.

A

Define the term overdraft [2]

The answer

One sentence, precise, no example needed. "An overdraft is a short-term source of external finance that allows a business to withdraw more money from its bank account than it holds, up to an agreed limit."

Length: one line. Time: under a minute.

B

Explain two sources of finance suitable for buying new machinery [4]

The answer

Two points, each developed. Name the source, say why it fits this particular purpose, then complete the reasoning. Machinery is a long-term asset, so a long-term source like a bank loan or leasing is appropriate, and you have to say why matching the term of the finance to the life of the asset matters.

Length: two short paragraphs. No evaluation.

C

Analyse the impact on the business of financing the machinery with a bank loan [6]

The answer

Now you need chains, not points. Interest raises fixed costs, which raises the break-even output, which is riskier if demand is uncertain. The stimulus will tell you whether it is. Both positive and negative consequences appear, tied to details of the case.

Length: three or four linked paragraphs. Still no verdict required.

D

Recommend a source of finance for the new machinery [10]

The answer

Everything above, plus a decision. You argue for one source, argue against the most credible alternative, then commit. Which source, on what condition, and what would change your mind. The judgement has to come from the case, so use the owner's objective, the firm's gearing, or how urgently the machine is needed.

Length: five or six paragraphs, ending in a judgement paragraph that is not a summary.

How to practise this

  1. Take one topic and write it at four levels, as above. This teaches the shapes far faster than answering four unrelated questions.
  2. Before writing anything, note the command term and the marks in the margin. Then sketch the skeleton, meaning how many points and whether you need a judgement, before the first sentence.
  3. Mark your own work against the shape, not the content. Ask whether you applied to the named organisation in every paragraph, and whether you answered the command term that was actually asked.

The full list of command terms and their official meanings is on the reference page.

How to study for a 7

Building a definition bank

Everything you learn in DP1 gets examined at the end of DP2, roughly eighteen months later. That constraint should decide how you take notes, and it rules out most of what students actually do.

Why definitions are worth building properly

Paper 1 Section A opens with recall questions worth [2] each. They are the most reliable marks in the subject. No application, no judgement, no case study reading. A student who can produce a precise one-sentence definition on demand banks those marks in three minutes and spends the saved time on the extended response.

There is a second effect that matters more. A definition you can state cleanly is a concept you can deploy. Vague knowledge produces vague application, and application is where the rest of the marks live.

The format

One row per term. Keep it in a spreadsheet or a table, one sheet per unit, because you will want to sort and filter it later.

TermExam definition, one sentenceAnchor exampleTopic
StakeholderAn individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities of a business.Suppliers of a café1.4
Working capitalThe funds available to a business for its day-to-day operations, calculated as current assets minus current liabilities.Cash to pay wages3.7
Market orientationAn approach in which a business bases its decisions on the needs and wants of consumers, identified through market research.Redesign after a survey4.1
  • Write the definition the way you would write it in the exam. Full sentence, in English, no bullet fragments. If you write notes to yourself in shorthand, shorthand is what you will produce under pressure.
  • One sentence, not three. A two-mark answer that runs five lines usually means you are not sure which part is the definition.
  • Keep the anchor example to four or five words. Its job is retrieval, not illustration.
  • Add terms the week you meet them. A bank built at the end of DP1 from a textbook is a copying exercise. One built weekly is revision.

The three-column note, for everything that is not a definition

Definitions cover recall. For concepts you will have to argue with, use three columns instead. The third column is where every evaluation mark in this subject comes from.

The conceptWhy a business would want itWhat it trades off against
Becoming a publicly held companyAccess to large amounts of share capital for expansionLoss of control, disclosure requirements, pressure for short-term returns
Penetration pricingFast market share in a competitive marketLow margins, a price consumers may anchor to, possible price war
Holding more stockFewer stock-outs and better service to customersCash tied up, storage costs, obsolescence risk
Why the third column

An evaluation question is asking you to weigh column two against column three for a specific business. If your notes only contain columns one and two, you have written a knowledge document, and a knowledge answer is all you will ever be able to produce from it.

Making DP1 content survive to DP2

  1. Close the book and write. Retrieval, not review. If you cannot produce the definition without looking, you have not learned it. Recognising a definition and generating one are different abilities, and only one of them gets examined.
  2. Revisit each unit's bank once a term, not once before the mock. Twenty minutes per unit spaced across the year beats an evening of rereading.
  3. Keep one page of concepts you keep confusing. Mission and vision. Publicly held and public sector. Profit and cash. Market share and market growth. These pairs are where examiners set traps, and the list is personal. Yours will not match anyone else's.
  4. Do not rewrite your notes to make them look better. Colour-coding and neat transcription feel productive and produce almost no retention. That time is better spent writing one [10] answer on an unseen case.
How to study for a 7

Answer templates and evaluation

Under time pressure you should not be inventing structure. Fix the shapes now, practise them until they are automatic, and spend your thinking in the exam on the case rather than on the paragraph.

The four-mark shape: point, application, explanation

Three moves in order, twice over, or once with more development.

Point. One source of finance suitable for [the organisation] is [the source].
Application. For [the organisation], this is appropriate because [a specific detail from the stimulus].
Explanation. This means that [first consequence], which in turn [second consequence].

The application sentence is what separates a 4 from a 2. It has to contain something that could only be true of the business in front of you. A number, a constraint, a stated objective, an industry detail. A sentence that would work for any business earns nothing.

The ten-mark shape

  1. Context. One sentence naming the organisation and the decision at hand. Not an introduction, just a bearing.
  2. Argument for. Point, application, explanation, developed into a chain.
  3. Argument against. The same structure for the strongest counter-argument, not the weakest one.
  4. Judgement. A decision, its strongest reason, and the condition it rests on.

Two developed sides and a real judgement will outscore four shallow points every time. At this level depth beats coverage.

What a judgement actually is

The most common ending in this subject is some version of "therefore it depends on the situation." That is not a judgement. It is a refusal to make one, and it scores accordingly.

A judgement is a decision made under a stated condition drawn from the case. It answers the question that was asked, it commits to a side, and it says what would change the answer.

On balance, [the organisation] should [action], because [the strongest single reason, tied to the case] outweighs [the main drawback], provided that [a condition visible in the stimulus]. If [that condition fails], [the alternative] would be preferable.

Practise this until you can write it in ninety seconds. It is the highest-value sentence pattern in the subject.

Six levers that turn a point into evaluation

When you need to weigh two things and cannot see how, reach for one of these. Each one gives you a legitimate basis for saying that one argument is stronger than another.

LeverThe question it asksExample phrasing
Time frameDoes the answer change in the short run versus the long run?"In the short run margins fall, but over three years the market share gained…"
StakeholderGood for whom? Who bears the cost?"Shareholders benefit, but employees face…"
MagnitudeHow big is the effect, relative to the business?"A $40,000 cost is significant for a firm with $250,000 revenue."
LikelihoodHow probable is the risk you are describing?"A price war is possible but unlikely given only two competitors…"
Opportunity costWhat is given up by choosing this?"Retained profit used here cannot fund the planned expansion."
ReversibilityHow easily can the decision be undone?"Leasing can be ended at renewal, but the purchase cannot."

Where the marks are actually lost

Five habits worth breaking in DP1
  • No application. The answer never names the organisation after the first line. Fix it by putting a case detail in every paragraph.
  • Both sides, no judgement. A balanced answer that just stops. Always write the judgement paragraph even if you are short on time, because it is worth more than another argument.
  • A judgement that repeats one side. "In conclusion, as explained above…" The judgement has to add the condition, not restate the reason.
  • Theory dumping. Everything you know about the seven Ps, in order, unasked. Pick the two or three the case turns on and go deep.
  • Answering the topic instead of the command term. Writing about stakeholders when asked to evaluate a decision's impact on them. Reread the command term after your second paragraph.
Unit 1

Introduction to Business Management

The vocabulary unit, and the one that supplies the evaluation engine you will use everywhere else. Stakeholders and business objectives come back in Finance, Marketing, Operations and the Internal Assessment.

1.11.21.31.41.51.6
1.1

What is a business?

The idea

A business is a decision-making organisation that combines the factors of production (land, labour, capital and enterprise) to produce goods or services. Businesses operate across the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors, and they organise themselves into functions: human resources, finance, marketing and operations.

This topic also covers why people start businesses and what makes it hard. Access to finance, competition, building a customer base, complying with regulation.

Exam definition
A business is a decision-making organisation that uses inputs, or factors of production, to produce goods and services which satisfy the needs and wants of customers.
In the exam
  • Identify the sector in which [organisation] operates. [2]
  • Explain two challenges [organisation] faced when it was set up. [4]
  • Describe two business functions relevant to [organisation]. [4]

Sector classification is a favourite opener because examiners can make it awkward. A coffee company that grows beans, roasts them and runs cafés spans all three main sectors.

How to write it

Never classify without justifying. "Secondary, because it converts raw coffee beans into roasted packaged coffee" earns the mark. "Secondary" on its own often does not. When a business spans sectors, say so and identify the dominant one, because that is usually what the mark scheme is built around.

On start-up challenges, avoid generic answers. Tie each challenge to something the stimulus tells you about this founder. Limited personal savings, an established competitor, a regulated industry.

1.2

Types of business entities

The idea

Sole traders, partnerships, privately held companies and publicly held companies. Then for-profit social enterprises (co-operatives, microfinance providers, public-private partnerships) and non-profit social enterprises (non-governmental organisations, charities).

Three distinctions do most of the analytical work: limited versus unlimited liability, ownership versus control, and which sources of finance the entity can access. Almost every question on this topic turns on one of them.

In the exam
  • Explain two advantages to [organisation] of becoming a privately held company. [4]
  • Recommend whether [organisation] should convert from a partnership to a privately held company. [10]
  • Distinguish between a privately held and a publicly held company. [4]

Social enterprise shows up here and again as the entire basis of Paper 3 at Higher Level, so do not treat it as a minor sub-heading.

How to write it

The recommendation question is where students list advantages and stop. The marks are in the fit between the legal structure and what the owner said they want. A founder who says she wants to keep creative control has just told you that share capital is expensive to her in a way it is not to someone chasing rapid expansion. Quote that and build the judgement on it.

For distinguish, write about both entities in every sentence. A paragraph on one followed by a paragraph on the other is not a distinction.

Trap
Publicly held is not public sector

A publicly held company is privately owned by shareholders and trades its shares on a stock exchange. A public sector organisation is owned by the government. Examiners use this pair on purpose. Students who confuse them write an entire answer about government ownership and score nothing.

1.3

Business objectives

The idea

Vision and mission statements, aims, objectives, strategic and tactical objectives, and the SMART criteria. Then the ethical side: corporate social responsibility, ethical objectives, and why objectives change as a business grows or its environment shifts.

Mission statement
A statement of a business's core purpose, meaning what it exists to do, in the present.
Vision statement
A statement of what a business aspires to become in the long-term future.
In the exam
  • Distinguish between a mission statement and a vision statement. [4]
  • Explain two reasons why [organisation] changed its objectives. [4]
  • Examine the impact of adopting an ethical objective on [organisation]. [10]
How to write it

Mission is present and purpose. Vision is future and aspiration. Hold that line and the distinguish question is free marks.

On corporate social responsibility, the weak answer says it improves reputation and stops there. The strong answer uses the time-frame lever. Costs land immediately and are measurable, while the differentiation and staff-retention benefits build slowly and are hard to quantify. That asymmetry is the evaluation.

1.4

Stakeholders

The idea

Internal stakeholders are employees, managers and shareholders. External stakeholders are customers, suppliers, government, pressure groups, competitors and the local community. Each group wants something, and what they want collides.

The conflict is the point. A decision that raises shareholder returns may cut employee hours. A decision that pleases customers may squeeze suppliers.

In the exam
  • Identify two external stakeholders of [organisation]. [2]
  • Explain one possible conflict between two stakeholder groups of [organisation]. [4]
  • Discuss the impact of [decision] on the stakeholders of [organisation]. [10]

This is the most reliable evaluation engine in the whole course. Any decision in any unit can be turned into a stakeholder question, which is why it keeps coming back.

How to write it

Pick two or three stakeholders and go deep. A ten-mark answer that lists six stakeholders with one sentence each looks thorough and scores badly, because nothing is developed.

For each one, name the stakeholder, state their specific interest, trace how the decision affects it, and say how strongly. Then use the stakeholder lever for your judgement. Whose interest should carry most weight here, and why does the case support that? A judgement that ranks stakeholders is far stronger than one that notes everyone is affected.

1.5

Growth and evolution

The idea

Economies and diseconomies of scale, both internal and external. Why small organisations survive alongside large ones. And how businesses grow, either organically or externally through mergers, acquisitions, takeovers, joint ventures, strategic alliances and franchising.

Internal economies of scale worth naming precisely: purchasing, technical, managerial, financial, marketing and risk-bearing.

In the exam
  • Explain two economies of scale [organisation] might gain from expansion. [4]
  • Recommend whether [organisation] should grow organically or through acquisition. [10]
  • Analyse two benefits to [organisation] of a joint venture. [6]
How to write it

Name the specific economy of scale, so "purchasing economies" rather than "economies of scale" in general, then connect it to this firm's cost per unit and to whether growth serves its objectives at all.

On the organic versus external question, the useful axes are speed, cost, risk and control. Organic growth is slower but cheaper and keeps the culture intact. Acquisition is fast but expensive and carries integration risk. Choose one, and condition the choice on something in the case. How urgent the expansion is, how much finance is available, how different the two cultures are.

Trap
Do not import the Economics answer

This is the biggest crossover error for students taking both subjects. There is no long-run average cost curve here and no diagram to draw. Business Management wants the named economy of scale, applied to a named firm, weighed against diseconomies and the owner's objectives. A technically flawless Economics explanation of economies of scale earns the knowledge mark and nothing further.

1.6

Multinational companies

The idea

The impact of multinational companies on the host countries they operate in, both the benefits and the costs. On one side, employment, tax revenue, technology and skills transfer, access to capital. On the other, profit repatriation, labour conditions, environmental damage, pressure on local firms and cultural effects.

In the exam
  • Explain two benefits to the host country of [organisation]'s new facility. [4]
  • Discuss the impact of [organisation]'s expansion into [country] on that host country. [10]

This topic turns up often as the Section B extended response, because it produces balance naturally.

How to write it

Split the impacts into economic and social or ethical and cover both. Answers that stay entirely economic, so jobs, tax and growth, read as half an answer.

For the judgement, the time-frame and stakeholder levers work best. Employment gains are immediate, while environmental costs and the displacement of local firms are slower. Local workers, local competitors and the host government are affected very differently, and saying so is what turns description into evaluation.

Unit 3

Finance and Accounts

The unit students fear and then over-prepare in the wrong direction. A formula booklet is provided in the examination, so the memorisation that actually pays is not formulas. It is the two financial statement layouts and the pairs of terms examiners use to catch you out.

Read this before anything else in Unit 3

You are given the ratio and appraisal formulas in the exam. What you are not given is the structure of the statement of profit or loss and the statement of financial position, and nobody hands you a glossary telling you that balance sheet and statement of financial position are the same document. Both are on the formula booklet page, and both are worth memorising before you memorise anything else here.

3.13.23.33.43.53.63.73.83.9
3.1

Introduction to finance

The idea

The role of finance in a business, and the distinction between capital expenditure, which is spending on non-current assets that will be used for more than a year, and revenue expenditure, which is spending on day-to-day running costs.

In the exam
  • Distinguish between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure. [4]
  • Identify one example of capital expenditure for [organisation]. [2]

Small on its own, but the classification underpins 3.2, 3.4 and 3.8, so getting it wrong propagates through several answers.

How to write it

Classify by the asset's useful life and say so: "a delivery van is capital expenditure because it will be used for several years." Watch the borderline cases. Repairing a machine is revenue expenditure, replacing it is capital expenditure.

3.2

Sources of finance

The idea

Internal: personal funds, retained profit, sale of assets. External: share capital, loan capital, overdrafts, trade credit, grants, subsidies, debt factoring, leasing, microfinance providers, business angels, crowdfunding.

Each source has a term (short, medium or long) and a cost, and each is available only to certain types of business entity.

In the exam
  • Explain two sources of finance available to [organisation]. [4]
  • Recommend a source of finance for [organisation]'s planned [investment]. [10]

The recommendation is one of the most predictable ten-mark questions in the whole course.

How to write it

Screen every candidate source against three filters before you write.

  1. Purpose. A long-term asset needs a long-term source. Financing machinery with an overdraft is a mismatch, and saying so earns marks.
  2. Entity type. A sole trader cannot issue shares. A privately held company cannot sell shares to the general public. Recommending an impossible source is fatal to the answer.
  3. Objective. Share capital dilutes control, loans preserve it but add fixed interest. Which cost matters more depends on what the owner said they want.

Then reject the alternatives out loud. An answer that argues for one source without saying why the obvious rival is worse has not evaluated.

3.3

Costs and revenues

The idea

Fixed, variable and semi-variable costs. Direct and indirect costs. Total costs and total revenue. Cost classification is the foundation for ratio work, budgeting, and break-even analysis over in Unit 5.

In the exam
  • Calculate the total costs of [organisation] for [period]. (Show all your working.) [2]
  • Distinguish between fixed and variable costs, using examples from the text. [4]

Classification traps are common. A supervisor paid a fixed monthly salary in a factory is a fixed and indirect cost, which catches students who assume anything in a factory is variable.

How to write it

Show working on every calculation, because the method carries marks even when the arithmetic goes wrong. State the formula, substitute the numbers, then give the answer with its currency or unit.

For classification, take your examples from the stimulus rather than inventing generic ones. That is the application mark.

Note
Break-even is not in this unit

Break-even analysis, contribution and margin of safety now sit in 5.5, Operations Management. Older textbooks and older notes put them in Finance. If your revision material has them here, it is out of date, and none of those formulas appear in the formula booklet either.

3.4

Final accounts

The idea

The two statements, meaning the statement of profit or loss and the statement of financial position, what each one shows, and why different stakeholders read them differently. Also intangible assets: goodwill, patents, copyrights and trademarks. At Higher Level, depreciation by the straight line and reducing balance methods.

Memorise the two layouts. They are not in the formula booklet, they are examined directly, and everything in 3.5 and 3.6 is built on knowing where each figure sits.

In the exam
  • Prepare a statement of profit or loss for [organisation] for the year ended [date]. [4]
  • Complete the statement of financial position by calculating [missing figures]. [4]
  • Explain why [stakeholder] would be interested in [organisation]'s final accounts. [4]

Prepare and complete are AO4 command terms. They test whether you can lay the statement out correctly, not whether you can discuss it.

How to write it

Use the full layout including the subtotals, even when the question only asks for one figure. The subtotal lines, meaning gross profit, profit before interest and tax, and net assets, are usually mark-bearing on their own.

On the statement of financial position, remember the check. Net assets must equal total equity. If they do not, you have made an error somewhere, and finding it takes seconds compared with the marks it costs.

When asked about a stakeholder's interest, name the figure they care about. A bank looks at gearing and liquidity, a supplier looks at the current ratio, an employee looks at profitability and its trend.

Trap
The same document, two names

Balance sheet = statement of financial position. Income statement = profit and loss account = statement of profit or loss. Examiners switch between the names on purpose, and a student who has only ever met one name freezes on a question that uses the other. The full list of paired terms, including stock and inventory, debtors and receivables, creditors and payables, is on the formula booklet page. Learn the pairs as pairs.

3.5

Profitability and liquidity ratio analysis

The idea

Profitability: gross profit margin, profit margin, return on capital employed. Liquidity: current ratio, acid test ratio. Plus the strategies a business can use to improve each one, and the drawbacks of those strategies.

All five formulas are in the booklet. The strategies and their drawbacks are not, and that is where the marks are.

In the exam
  • Calculate the gross profit margin for [year]. (Show all your working.) [2]
  • Comment on the change in [organisation]'s liquidity position between [years]. [4]
  • Analyse two strategies [organisation] could use to improve its profitability. [6]
How to write it

The number is one mark. The comment is the rest. Work through four moves.

  1. Calculate. Formula, substitution, answer with the percentage sign or "times".
  2. Compare. To the previous year, or to whatever benchmark the stimulus gives you.
  3. Explain the change using something the case tells you. A new supplier, a price cut, a marketing campaign.
  4. Say what follows. A strategy, and its cost.

Every improvement strategy has a price. Raising prices to lift the gross profit margin risks volume. Cutting cost of sales by changing supplier risks quality. Naming that trade-off is what moves an answer from analysis into evaluation.

3.6

Debt/equity ratio analysis

HL
The idea

The gearing ratio, which is non-current liabilities as a percentage of capital employed, measures how much of a firm's long-term funding comes from debt rather than equity. It is in the formula booklet. Its interpretation is not.

In the exam
  • Calculate the gearing ratio for [organisation]. [2]
  • Comment on [organisation]'s gearing and its implications for future borrowing. [4]
How to write it

Resist "high gearing is bad". High gearing does mean higher risk, since interest has to be paid whatever profits do, but debt is generally cheaper than equity and it does not dilute the owners' control. Whether it is a problem depends on how stable this firm's cash flows are, and the stimulus will tell you.

Link it forward. High existing gearing is a strong argument against recommending a bank loan in a 3.2 question. Examiners set these two up together.

3.7

Cash flow

The idea

The difference between profit and cash. Working capital. Constructing and interpreting a cash flow forecast. The causes of cash flow problems, such as overtrading, poor credit control, seasonal demand and holding too much stock, and the strategies that fix them.

Working capital
The funds available to a business for its day-to-day operations, calculated as current assets minus current liabilities.
In the exam
  • Construct a cash flow forecast for [organisation] for [period]. [4]
  • Explain why [organisation] is profitable but has a cash flow problem. [4]
  • Recommend two strategies to improve [organisation]'s cash flow position. [10]
How to write it

Profit is not cash, and that idea is the most tested one in this topic. It has a standard explanation worth having ready. A sale made on credit is recorded as revenue immediately but the cash arrives later, so a business can be profitable on paper and still unable to pay its wages this month.

On improvement strategies, every fix has a cost you have to name. Delaying payment to suppliers damages the relationship and may cost you credit terms. Debt factoring produces cash immediately but at a discount to the invoice value. Cutting stock risks losing sales. The strategy without its cost is analysis. The strategy with its cost is evaluation.

3.8

Investment appraisal

The idea

Payback period, meaning how long until the initial investment is recovered. Average rate of return, the annual profit as a percentage of the initial cost. At Higher Level, net present value, which discounts future returns to what they are worth today.

Average rate of return and net present value are in the formula booklet. Payback period is not.

In the exam
  • Calculate the payback period for [investment]. [2]
  • Calculate the average rate of return for both options. [4]
  • Recommend which investment [organisation] should undertake. [10]

The standard setup gives you two options with conflicting signals. One pays back faster, the other returns more overall. That conflict is the question.

How to write it

Numbers alone never win a recommendation. State what each method says, point out that they disagree, then decide using qualitative factors from the case. How tight is cash flow, how uncertain is demand, does the owner need a fast return, does one option fit the stated objectives better?

Name the limitations of the methods you used. Payback period ignores everything that happens after the payback point. Average rate of return ignores the timing of returns entirely. A student who says so is demonstrating exactly the critical thinking the subject rewards.

3.9

Budgets

HL
The idea

Cost centres and profit centres, and variance analysis. A variance is the difference between a budgeted figure and the actual figure, described as favourable when it improves profit and adverse when it reduces it.

In the exam
  • Calculate the variance for [item] and state whether it is favourable or adverse. [2]
  • Explain two benefits to [organisation] of using cost centres. [4]
  • Comment on the variances shown in the table. [4]
How to write it

Always label the variance favourable or adverse, because the number alone is usually half the mark. The direction depends on the line. Costs below budget are favourable, revenue below budget is adverse.

When commenting, look for the causal story across variances instead of reading each one in isolation. Marketing spend above budget alongside revenue above budget is a different situation from marketing spend above budget with revenue on target, and saying which one you are looking at is the interpretation mark.

Unit 4

Marketing

The unit where students write the most and score the least, because the content feels intuitive. Marketing questions are almost always recommend or evaluate, and intuition without application does not earn those marks.

4.14.24.34.44.54.6
4.1

Introduction to marketing

The idea

Marketing and how it relates to the other business functions. Market orientation versus product orientation. Commercial marketing versus social marketing. And the three quantitative measures: market share, market growth and market leadership.

Market share
A firm's sales revenue expressed as a percentage of total sales revenue in the market.
Market growth
The percentage increase in the size of a market over a given period.
In the exam
  • Calculate [organisation]'s market share in [year]. [2]
  • Distinguish between market orientation and product orientation. [4]
How to write it

Keep market share and market growth apart, because they are a favourite confusion. Share is your slice. Growth is the size of the whole pie. A firm can lose share in a growing market and still increase its sales, and a question that hands you both figures is usually testing exactly that.

Product orientation is not automatically the wrong choice. For a business selling a genuine technical innovation it can be defensible, and saying so is a stronger answer than a blanket claim that market orientation is superior.

4.2

Marketing planning

The idea

The elements of a marketing plan. Segmentation (demographic, geographic, psychographic), targeting and positioning. Position or perception maps. The unique selling point. And the difference between a marketing objective and a marketing strategy.

In the exam
  • Construct a position map showing [organisation] and its competitors. [4]
  • Explain two ways [organisation] segments its market. [4]
  • Recommend a marketing strategy for [organisation]'s new product. [10]
How to write it

On a position map, label both axes with the attributes the stimulus emphasises, usually price against quality but not always, and plot every firm named in the case. Marks are lost for unlabelled axes far more often than for misplaced points.

Segmentation answers have to use the case's own segments. "They segment by age" is generic. "They segment by age, targeting the 18 to 25 group identified in the survey" is the application mark.

An objective is a destination, like "increase market share to 15% within two years". A strategy is how you get there. Mixing them up in a recommendation question costs you the framing of the whole answer.

4.3

Sales forecasting

HL
The idea

Moving averages up to four parts, and the variations they help expose: seasonal, cyclical and random. Plus the benefits and limitations of forecasting sales at all.

In the exam
  • Calculate the four-part moving average for [period]. [4]
  • Explain two limitations of sales forecasting for [organisation]. [4]

This is the most calculation-heavy topic in Unit 4, and the arithmetic is where students lose marks rather than the concept.

How to write it

Set the calculation out in a table with a column for each step, and centre four-part averages correctly. The classic error is an off-by-one alignment that then wrecks every figure after it.

On limitations, tie them to the industry in the case. Forecasting from historical data is weak in a fast-changing market and useless for a genuinely new product with no history, so say which situation this business is in.

4.4

Market research

The idea

Primary and secondary research. Qualitative and quantitative data. Sampling methods, meaning quota, random, stratified, cluster, snowballing and convenience. Analysing results, and the ethical considerations of researching consumers.

In the exam
  • Identify the sampling method used by [organisation]. [2]
  • Explain two limitations of the market research [organisation] carried out. [4]
  • Recommend a market research method for [organisation]'s new product. [10]
How to write it

Limitations have to be specific to the method described. "The sample was too small" is generic. "A convenience sample taken outside one store cannot represent the national market they intend to enter" uses both the method and the case.

For the recommendation, weigh cost against how reliable the data will be, and anchor it to what the business can actually afford. A start-up with limited funds and a national ambition is a different problem from an established firm testing a line extension.

4.5

The seven Ps of the marketing mix

The idea

The largest topic in the unit, and the one most likely to carry the extended response.

  • Product. The product life cycle, extension strategies, the Boston Consulting Group matrix.
  • Price. Cost-plus, penetration, skimming, psychological, loss leader. At Higher Level also price discrimination, price leadership and predatory pricing.
  • Promotion. Above and below the line, the promotional mix, guerrilla marketing, social media and viral marketing.
  • Place. Distribution channels and the trade-off between reach and margin.
  • People, Processes, Physical evidence. The service extension, which matters most for service businesses.
In the exam
  • Identify the stage of the product life cycle [product] has reached. [2]
  • Explain two extension strategies [organisation] could use. [4]
  • Recommend changes to [organisation]'s marketing mix. [10]
How to write it

Never write about all seven. A ten-mark answer covering seven Ps gives each one about thirty words, and none of them can be developed or applied. Pick the two or three the case actually turns on, and say in your first line why those are the ones that matter. That sentence is itself an evaluative move.

The mix has to be internally consistent, and pointing that out is a strong evaluation lever. Premium pricing alongside discount-retailer distribution is incoherent, and a recommendation that changes one P without checking the others is incomplete.

On the product life cycle, justify the stage from the data. Falling sales with rising competition points to decline. Slowing sales growth with the highest profits points to maturity.

4.6

International marketing

HL
The idea

Methods of entering an international market, the opportunities and threats of doing so, and the cultural differences that force a business to adapt its marketing mix.

In the exam
  • Explain two cultural factors [organisation] should consider before entering [market]. [4]
  • Discuss whether [organisation] should adapt or standardise its marketing mix abroad. [10]

Often combined with 1.6 on multinational companies, since the two topics share their subject matter.

How to write it

The adapt versus standardise question has a real trade-off at its centre. Standardisation gives economies of scale and a consistent brand. Adaptation gives local relevance at a higher cost. Choose based on how culturally distant the target market is and how price-sensitive its consumers are.

Avoid vague cultural generalisations. Use whatever specifics the stimulus gives you, such as language, religious observance, buying habits or regulation, rather than asserting that a country is "more traditional".
